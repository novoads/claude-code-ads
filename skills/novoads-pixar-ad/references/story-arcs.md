# Story arcs

Five arcs that fit the 15-second skeleton. Pick one before writing a word of script — the
arc decides what the low point is, and the low point is the two seconds the ad lives or
dies on.

Each carries a **trap**, because the failure mode is specific to the arc rather than
general. An ad that goes wrong usually went wrong at the arc, not at the render.

---

## 1. The Withheld Ordinary

**Shape.** A character wants something small and universal. They cannot have it. The
product returns it.

**Low point.** They stop trying, quietly, and nobody sees.

**Best for.** Accessibility, comfort, sleep, mobility, reading, hearing. Anything that
restores a capability rather than adding one.

**Why it converts.** The want is pre-agreed. Nobody needs persuading that reading to a
grandchild is worth wanting, so all 15 seconds go to the feeling instead of the argument.

**Trap.** Pity. The character must want the thing, not be defined by lacking it. Play the
reaching, not the deprivation, or the ad reads as a charity appeal and the product becomes
a consolation prize.

---

## 2. The Small Rebellion

**Shape.** A character is quietly failing at a rule everyone accepts. The product lets them
opt out of the rule rather than get better at it.

**Low point.** They comply one more time, and it costs them.

**Best for.** Category disruption, "you were sold the wrong thing", any product whose pitch
is *stop doing it the hard way*.

**Why it converts.** It reframes the viewer's failure as a bad rule rather than a personal
one, which is a far easier thing to accept in three seconds.

**Trap.** Naming a competitor, even obliquely. The rule is the antagonist, never a brand.
Attack the practice, and let the audience assign the blame.

---

## 3. The Gift That Lands

**Shape.** Someone buys for someone else. The gift almost misses. It lands.

**Low point.** The purchaser watches, unsure, in the half-second before the reaction.

**Best for.** Anything where reviews show the buyer is not the user — adult children buying
for parents, partners buying for partners.

**Why it converts.** It targets the person holding the card and gives them the exact
emotional payoff they are shopping for. Gate 1's "who actually buys" question exists for
this arc.

**Trap.** Making the gift about the product's features. The purchaser's face at the moment
of reaction is the shot; the product is the instrument. If the camera is on the product at
0:11, the arc is broken.

---

## 4. The Companion

**Shape.** An object is present through a stretch of ordinary life. Nothing dramatic
happens. It is simply always there.

**Low point.** A moment where it would have been needed and was absent.

**Best for.** Doctrine D products with real articulation, durable goods, daily-carry items,
anything whose pitch is reliability rather than transformation.

**Why it converts.** Reliability is almost impossible to argue and easy to show. Fifteen
seconds of quiet presence outperforms any durability claim you would have to defend.

**Trap.** No stakes. Without the absence beat at 0:06 this becomes a product beauty reel
with a voice-over. The low point must show the cost of not having it.

---

## 5. The Handoff

**Shape.** Two characters, one older and one younger. Something passes between them, and
the product is how.

**Low point.** The older character cannot do the thing they used to.

**Best for.** Heritage, craft, teaching, family, anything with a "generations" claim that
would sound cloying spoken aloud.

**Why it converts.** It carries a longevity claim entirely in the image, so the VO never has
to say "built to last" — which Gate 1 may well have forbidden anyway.

**Trap.** Two speaking characters plus a narrator inside a 33-word budget. In this arc give
SYNC to one character only, and let the other answer with an action.

---

## Choosing between them

| If the product… | Use |
|---|---|
| restores something the buyer used to be able to do | The Withheld Ordinary |
| replaces an accepted-but-bad practice | The Small Rebellion |
| is mostly bought for someone else | The Gift That Lands |
| is bought for reliability, and has real articulation | The Companion |
| carries a heritage or teaching claim | The Handoff |

Two arcs fitting equally well means the product read is not specific enough yet. Go back to
Gate 1 and find who actually buys — that answer usually picks the arc for you.

---

## Worked example — The Gift That Lands

Product: a lamp with a genuinely large, high-contrast dial. Reviews show adult children
buying it for a parent.

| TC | Track | Line | Visual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:03 | SYNC | "I'll just read it tomorrow." | Older woman sets a book down, unopened. Warm lamp, dial too small to see. |
| 0:03–0:06 | VO | Some evenings quietly stop being yours. | She reaches for the dial, misses it twice. |
| 0:06–0:08 | SYNC | *(silent)* | Her hand comes to rest on the closed cover. The low point. |
| 0:08–0:11 | SYNC | "Try this one, Mum." | Daughter sets the new lamp down. One frame: the page is legible. |
| 0:11–0:15 | VO | Light you can actually reach. | She opens the book. Hard cut at 0:13 to hero card. |

**Word count: 31.** Inside the 30-to-33 budget.

Note what the ad does not do: no lumens, no dial diameter, no comparison. The claim is
carried entirely by the single-frame change at 0:09, and the daughter's line is the only
place the product is named.
